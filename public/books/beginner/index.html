Meet the Microduck

How a little robot duck senses, thinks, moves and learns. Ten lessons, each with a pop-up to play.

Who it is for: readers 7 and up, with a grown-up. There is no code, and you do not need a robot: paper Duck Cards, Microduck Studio and Kith are enough.

  1. A body, senses and a brain
  2. Do this, then that

  5. Why walking is hard
  6. Learning by trying
  7. A practice world
  8. Pretend is not perfect
  9. Kind company
  10. Your duck, your idea

How a lesson works: read the left page and make a prediction. Then press the steps on the right page, one at a time, and find out if you were right. Each lesson ends with things to try for real, notes for grown-ups, and where every number comes from.

Lesson 1 · The robot

A body, senses and a brain

The Microduck is a little robot duck, a little shorter than a school ruler is long.

It has a body: two legs to walk on, a long neck, and a head with a beak.

It has senses. A sensor inside its body feels which way is down, a bit like the feeling in your tummy on a swing. And every motor can feel how far it has turned.

It has a brain: a program on a tiny computer inside it. The brain reads the senses, decides what to do, and tells the motors to move. Then it does it all again. And again.

Sense, think, move. Then do it again.

Predict

How many times does the duck sense, think and move in one second?

Check your prediction

50 times every second. That is faster than you can blink, so the duck can feel a wobble and fix it before it turns into a fall.

Try it for real

Be the loop. One person is the senses and calls out "tipping left!" or "tipping right!". One is the brain and decides. One is the motors and moves a toy's legs. How many loops can you do in 10 seconds? The duck does 500.

No robot needed to see one move: Microduck Studio drives a duck in physics on an iPhone, iPad or Mac.

For parents and teachers

Sense, think, act is one of the oldest ideas in robotics, and it is the shape of everything later in these books. The duck has 15 motors: 14 for its legs, neck and head, which its walking brain moves, and one that opens its beak.

The paper duck in the pictures is our own drawing with the robot's joints, not Pollen Robotics' design.

Where these numbers come from

  • 50 a second: the robot's code runs "a 50 Hz control loop driving fifteen servos" (Pollen Robotics' microduck README), and its walking brains "are trained here at 50 Hz" (microduck_rl README).
  • 14 and 15 motors: "Joints exclude the mouth throughout" (robotd design), and the training code's "Joint layout (14 servos)".
  • 500 is 50 times 10.

Lesson 2 · Duck Cards

Do this, then that

A robot does what it is told, in the order it is told. Duck Cards are one way to tell it. Each card is one thing the duck can do.

Lay cards in a row and read them from left to right. That row is a program: a plan the duck follows, one card at a time.

On the paper board, one Walk forward card moves the duck one square. One Turn card turns it a quarter turn, on the spot.

One Walk card is one square. One Turn card is a quarter turn.

Predict

The duck is in the bottom left corner, facing up. The pond is one square up and two squares to the right. How many Walk and Turn cards get it there, at the fewest?

Check your prediction

4 cards: Walk forward, Turn right, Walk forward, Walk forward. One card to go up, one to turn, and two to go across. A turn is a card too.

Try it for real

Print the starter deck and play Be the duck: one person is the duck and does exactly what the cards say, and nothing else.

Then play the pack Get to the pond, six challenges on the same small board. Tape the squares on the floor so the person playing the duck can stand in one.

For parents and teachers

Let the child build the whole row before anything moves. A wrong row is the best part: ask "where did the duck go that you did not mean?" That question is debugging.

Turn cards trip almost everyone at first, because a turn moves the duck nowhere. Standing up and turning on the spot settles it.

Where these numbers come from

  • The board rule, "One Walk card moves the duck one square. One Turn card is a quarter turn, where it stands" (Duck Card packs).
  • The board of 3 by 3 squares is the size of Get to the pond.
  • 4 cards: counted by playing the row on the board.

Lesson 3 · Loops

Say it shorter

Some programs do the same thing again and again. To walk round a square, the duck walks forward twice and turns, then does that again, and again, and again.

Written out card by card, that is a long row of 12 cards. Long rows are hard to read and easy to get wrong.

A Repeat card says it shorter. Write on it how many times. Put the cards to repeat after it, and close them with End repeat. Everything between the two is a loop.

A loop does the cards inside it again and again.

Predict

How many cards does the short row need, counting the Repeat and End repeat cards?

Check your prediction

5 cards: Repeat 4 times, Walk forward, Walk forward, Turn left, End repeat. The duck walks the very same square with 7 fewer cards.

Try it for real

Do the deck's lesson Say it shorter: write a dance that does a few cards again and again, then rewrite it with Repeat and End repeat and count the cards you saved.

The pack Loop the course is scored by fewest cards, so loops pay off there.

For parents and teachers

Ask what changes if the dance repeats once more: in the long row it is three more cards, in the loop it is one number. That is why programmers love loops.

A loop also keeps a bug in one place. Put a wrong card inside the loop and the duck repeats the mistake four times, which makes it easy to spot, and there is one card to fix.

Where these numbers come from

  • 12 cards: three cards for each of the four sides. 5 cards: counted in the short row. 7 fewer is 12 take away 5.
  • The square, 2 squares on a side, fits the board of 4 by 4 squares drawn here, the size used by the pack Duck maze.

Lesson 4 · Events

When it falls

Walking robots fall. A good program says what to do when that happens.

A "When it falls" card starts a second row. That row waits. It does nothing at all until the duck falls over.

The real Microduck already knows how to get up. Knock it over and it stands itself up again.

A When row waits for something to happen. Then it runs.

Predict

The duck is halfway through its first row when a ball knocks it over. Once it is back on its feet, what does it do?

Check your prediction

It does its "When it falls" row. Once the duck is back on its feet, the second row runs. The rest of the first row does not run. The second row was waiting the whole time, and a fall is the thing it was waiting for.

Try it for real

Play the deck's lesson When it falls. The person playing the duck sits down when you say "fall", gets up, and does the second row.

Then argue about it: should the duck do the same thing after every fall?

For parents and teachers

Programmers call a When row an event handler. Phones are full of them: when a message arrives, when the screen is tapped.

Getting up after a fall is not a card. It is a skill the real robot learned: Pollen Robotics trained one brain that walks and recovers from falls, and another that stands up from lying face down, face up or sitting.

Where these numbers come from

No numbers in this lesson. "Knock it over and it stands itself up" is from Pollen Robotics' microduck README; the trained skills are the VelStand and StandUp tasks in the microduck_rl README.

Lesson 5 · Balance

Why walking is hard

Stand on one foot. Feel the wobble? You are balancing.

Every body has a middle point, where all its weight seems to sit. It is called the center of mass. To stay up, that point has to stay above your feet.

The duck's head is heavy: more than a third of the whole duck. When its head swings, its middle point moves, and the duck has to keep it over its feet.

Walking is even harder. A walking body moves its middle point ahead of its feet on purpose, starts to fall, and catches itself with the next step. Over and over.

Keep your middle point over your feet.

Predict

The duck leans forward more and more. When does it tip over?

Check your prediction

When its middle point passes its toes. Leaning is fine while the middle point is still over the feet. Past the toes, nothing is holding it up.

Try it for real

Hang a string with a weight on it, a key on a shoelace works, from a toy's middle. Lean the toy until the string passes the edge of its feet, and it falls.

Then walk across the room very slowly. Notice the moment you start to fall forward, and your foot arriving to catch you.

For parents and teachers

The ground the feet cover is called the support area. Standing still, the middle point has to stay above it. Walking is harder, because the duck spends part of every step on one foot.

The duck's heavy head matters a lot. Its trainers found that the head must swing while it walks, and when they punished every little head wobble, the duck learned to stand still instead of walking.

Where these numbers come from

  • More than a third: the training code says the head is "280 g" and "38% of robot mass" (the walking task's settings).
  • The duck that stood still: the same file, "Standing still scored higher, so it stood still."

Lesson 6 · Learning

Learning by trying

How did you learn to ride a bike? Nobody could tell you exactly what to do with every muscle. You tried, you wobbled, and slowly your body worked it out.

Robots can learn like that too. It is called reinforcement learning. The robot tries something. When it goes well, it gets points, called a reward. After many, many tries, it does more of whatever earned points.

Nobody tells it how. It finds out.

Try. Get points for good tries. Do more of what worked.

Predict

A duck in a little maze gets a point each time it reaches the pond. At first it wanders about. After many tries, what does it do?

Check your prediction

It finds the short way to the pond. Its first lucky path wandered, but every try that reached the pond left a little trail of "this was good". After enough tries, the trail leads straight there.

Try it for real

Play hot and cold. One person hides a toy. The seeker moves one step at a time, and the others say only "warmer" or "colder". That is a reward, and the seeker is learning from it.

Then play it with a rule: "warmer" only when the seeker finds the toy. It takes much longer. Rewards that come early help learning.

For parents and teachers

This maze learns with the simplest kind of reinforcement learning, where every square keeps a number for how good it is to stand there. The real duck cannot keep a number for every position of every joint, so it learns a small network instead. Book 2 opens it up.

The points in the picture are made up for the toy. The real duck's points are in Book 2, lesson 5.

Where these numbers come from

No real numbers here: the maze is a toy, and its points are ours.

Lesson 7 · Simulation

A practice world

Learning by trying takes an enormous number of tries, and an enormous number of falls. A real duck would wear out.

So the Microduck practises in a pretend world inside a computer, called a simulation. There, falling does not hurt anything, and the pretend duck can be reset in a blink.

Better still, the computer can run 4,096 pretend ducks.

A fast computer can teach the pretend ducks a walk that works in about 1 to 2 hours.

Practise in pretend first, with lots of ducks at once.

Predict

Why practise with 4,096 ducks instead of just one?

Check your prediction

So one brain learns from all of them. All 4,096 ducks try at the same time. Every one of them tries, falls and learns, and all of them share one brain. That brain learns from all their tries at once, so it gets its practice thousands of times faster.

Try it for real

Studio's Learn tab is nine short lessons, from driving the duck to how it is trained, each with a button that opens the real screen.

A grown-up with a computer that has an NVIDIA graphics card can start this exact practice with Pollen Robotics' microduck_rl. Book 2, lesson 6 shows how.

For parents and teachers

The pretend world is called MuJoCo, a physics engine that works out how bodies push, fall and slide. The trick that makes 4,096 ducks affordable is a graphics chip, which is built to do thousands of small sums at once.

"A walk that works" is honest about what 1 to 2 hours buys: a usable walk, not the best one.

Where these numbers come from

  • 4,096 ducks and 1 to 2 hours: "~1-2 h for a usable gait at 4096 envs" (microduck_rl README). An env is one pretend duck in its pretend world.
  • Nine lessons in Learn: Studio's docs.

Lesson 8 · Sim to real

Pretend is not perfect

The pretend world is close to the real one, but it is not the same. A real floor can be more slippery. A real battery gets tired. Every real motor is a tiny bit different from the next.

So while the ducks practise, the pretend world is shaken up on purpose. Some ducks get slippery floors and some get grippy ones. Every duck gets a little push now and then. A duck that learned on all of them is ready for a floor it has never met.

Practise on many different pretend worlds, so the real one is just one more.

Predict

A duck that practised in the pretend world walks a row of 4 Walk cards on a floor it has never met. Each card is meant to go one square. Where does it end up?

Check your prediction

Near the fourth square, a little off. Even after all that practice, a real duck slowly wanders off its path. Each step goes a little wrong, and the error grows the longer it walks. The longer the row, the farther off it ends. For now, a Walk card on a real duck is timed: it walks for two seconds, and it does not land exactly on a square.

Try it for real

Walk across the room with your eyes closed, counting 4 steps, and mark where you stop. Do it 5 times. Your marks spread out, just like the duck's.

Tape the paper board on the floor in squares of 30 cm, so the person playing the duck can stand in one.

For parents and teachers

Shaking up the pretend world is called domain randomization. Each pretend duck gets its own floor grip, body weight and sensor errors for the whole of training. Its balance point, its motors' strength and little delays in its senses change again each time it starts over, and every duck gets pushed now and then.

Microduck Studio tested cards meant to walk one square, in Pollen Robotics' own simulator, and published every result. Short rows nearly pass; long rows do not yet, because small errors add up with time. The evidence page has every number.

Where these numbers come from

  • "Walk and Turn cards on a duck are timed for now: two seconds each, and the duck does not land on board squares" (Duck Card packs). The squares of 30 cm are from the same page.
  • 4 cards and 5 tries are the activity's own counts.

Lesson 9 · Living with people

Kind company

A robot that lives with people needs more than legs. It needs good manners.

Kith is a game you play in a web browser, made by the same person as this site. You walk with Microducks through a paper valley, or along a real park path. In Kith's park, the ducks keep the real robot's pace, as measured in simulation. It is slow: about as fast as a very old dog. A good friend slows down to walk with you.

Good manners are skills too, and the duck can practise them.

Keep pace, and wait for each other.

Predict

The duck comes to steps it cannot climb. What is the clever, kind thing for it to do?

Check your prediction

Stop and ask for help. In Kith the duck stops and asks with a quack. The person lifts it over the steps and sets it down, and they carry on together. Waiting, asking and helping are all skills the duck can practise: keep pace, wait, ask for help, and help back.

Try it for real

Play Kith in a web browser. Walk as the traveler, or choose to walk as a Microduck and see the world at its pace.

Then go for a walk with someone much slower than you, a small child or an old dog. What did you do differently?

For parents and teachers

Kith gives points for keeping company: a little every moment the duck stays near the traveler. Walks people choose to give become an open set of examples a real Microduck could learn from.

Kith has no chat and no names: everyone is renamed in what it records, and anyone can choose not to share their play.

Where these numbers come from

  • The pace: the real robot as it walks today, "measured in simulation (about 0.12 m/s on the level and 0.15 at most...)". The old dog walks "0.14 m/s, a Microduck's own pace" (Kith's notes for agents).
  • The points for company are listed at Kith's rewards.

Lesson 10 · Making

Your duck, your idea

The best Duck Cards are the ones you invent. The deck comes with blank cards, for moves you make up. Each one has its own little square code.

To make a move, pose the duck a few times. Each pose is a keyframe: where every joint should be, and when. The duck moves smoothly from one keyframe to the next.

Every joint has a limit, like your elbow. A good move stays inside the limits. Microduck Studio checks every angle in a move against the real duck's joints.

A move is poses in time, inside the joints' limits.

Predict

You try to tip the duck's head sideways much farther than it can go. What happens?

Check your prediction

The head stops at its limit. The duck's head can tip about 20 degrees each way. Studio checks every angle in a move against the robot's joints, so it never asks for more. The robot's own software only catches broken orders, so staying inside the limits is the move's job.

Try it for real

In Studio's Play screen, Mimic puts the duck in the pose of whoever stands in front of the camera. Strike a pose, keep it as a motion, then draw a card for it and give it a name.

Share your class's deck back: photograph it and open an issue on the site's repository.

For parents and teachers

A keyframe animation does not know about gravity. Studio's preview "interpolates your poses and times; it does not calculate whether the robot could perform the move", so a move can look fine and still topple a real duck. The physics bench answers that question in simulation.

The robot's safety code holds each motor inside its range of travel, which is not the same as what is safe for each joint. So a good move stays inside the joints' limits itself. Its trainers never ask the head to go all the way, either: their head commands stop about a tenth short of each joint's real range.

Where these numbers come from

  • About 20 degrees: "head_roll : ±0.31 rad (limit ±20°)", and the commands' "~10% safety margin" (the walking task's settings). The robot's control loop will "clamp to actuator range" (robotd design).
  • Studio checks angles: "Every angle it writes is checked against the robot's own joint travel before you see it" (Microduck Studio). The preview sentence is from the docs.
