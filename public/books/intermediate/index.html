How a Microduck Learns

How does a duck learn to walk when nobody writes the walk? Ten lessons, with the real numbers.

Who it is for: teens and adults, and anyone who finished Book 1. You never need to write code, though a few notes show commands for you or a friend who codes. Every number has a source, most of them Pollen's training code, and the notes say where.

  1. Observe, act, score, repeat

  3. 14 motors, 14 numbers
  4. A brain made of sums
  5. Points for what you want
  6. Practice, at scale
  7. Small steps uphill
  8. Train on many worlds

How a lesson works: commit to a prediction on the left page, then run the steps on the right one at a time. The notes go further, into what to try in Microduck Studio, Kith or Pollen Robotics' training code and where every number comes from.

Lesson 1 · The loop

Observe, act, score, repeat

Every reinforcement learning problem has the same shape. An agent, here the duck's brain, gets an observation of the world and picks an action. The world moves on, and hands back the next observation and a reward: one number for how good that moment was.

The duck's loop runs 50 times a second. One turn of it is a step, and the steps from a reset until the duck falls, or time runs out, make an episode.

The agent is not trying to win one step but to collect as much reward as it can from the steps ahead, counting near ones more than far ones. With Pollen's settings about the next 2 seconds count most, which is why a duck learns to take a small loss now, like a careful step, for more later.

observation → action → next observation and reward → again

Predict

Suppose an episode lasts 20 seconds. How many decisions does the duck make in it?

Check your prediction

1,000: 50 decisions a second for 20 seconds, each one an observation in and an action out, and each earning a reward.

Try it for real

In Microduck Studio, open Behaviours, choose a policy Released by Pollen Robotics, and use Probe this network, which runs the middle of one turn of this loop: an observation in, actions out.

In Kith, a web game by this site's author, you can walk as a Microduck. Kith can record the walk as an episode: five rows a second of what the duck sensed, the action it took next, and its reward.

For parents and teachers

The words in bold are the whole vocabulary of reinforcement learning. A good check is to ask a student to name the agent, the observation, the action and the reward in a game they play.

A picture for the 2 seconds: a cyclist steers by the road a few metres ahead rather than the patch under the front wheel, and those seconds are the duck's few metres.

A reset is the simulator putting the duck back on its feet. A real robot cannot reset in a blink: someone, or its stand-up skill, has to get it up, and every fall wears it. That is one reason the learning happens in simulation (lesson 6).

Where these numbers come from

  • 50 steps a second: "Policies are trained here at 50 Hz" (microduck_rl README).
  • The 20-second episode is a round number for the question. 1,000 is 50 times 20.

Lesson 2 · The observation

61 numbers

The duck's brain never sees the room, only a list of 61 numbers, 50 times a second.

3 say how fast its body is turning (a gyroscope) and 3 which way is down. 14 say where each joint is, 14 how fast each is moving, and 14 repeat the actions the brain chose last step. The last 13 are its orders: how fast to walk and turn (3), where to point its head (4), and how to hold its body (6).

Every walking, standing and trick brain Pollen Robotics trains reads this same list in the same order, so the robot can hand over from one brain to another, say from walking to a kick.

Some things are missing on purpose: the robot has no direct way to measure its own speed, and the ball-kicking brain never sees the ball.

3 + 3 + 14 + 14 + 14 + 13 = 61

Predict

Why might the list include the duck's own last actions?

Check your prediction

Its joints lag behind. A joint takes time to reach its target, so the joint readings alone are always a little behind, but its last orders say where the joints are heading. They fill in the rest, and they also let the brain keep its moves smooth.

Try it for real

In Studio's Probe this network, start from an observation preset and change one input. The z-score shows how far it sits from what the network saw in training, and the sensitivity ranking shows which inputs move its outputs most.

For parents and teachers

A useful exercise is to list what you would want to know to keep your balance, then cross out everything a small robot cannot measure. What is left is close to this list.

In the body pose, x, y and yaw are always zero on the robot today, and the rest are zero unless body pose is in use. The slots stay anyway, so that one layout fits every brain.

Where these numbers come from

  • The layout: "61-dim actor obs: 48 proprioception + commands [twist(3), head_pose(4), body_pose(6)]" (microduck_rl README), and the robot's own "[ gyro(3) | projected_gravity(3) | joint_pos(14) | joint_vel(14) | last_action(14) | command(13) ]" (robotd design).
  • Swapping brains: the runtime "hot-swaps these policies (walk / recover / trick) behind a shared 61-dimensional observation contract". The kicker is "BLIND to the ball" (the ball kick task).

Lesson 3 · The action

14 motors, 14 numbers

Each step, the brain answers with 14 numbers, one per motor, always in the same order: the left leg's 5 joints, then 4 in the neck and head, then the right leg's 5.

A number is not a speed or a push. It is a target, where the joint should go, measured from a home pose. The motor's own small controller then drives the joint toward it, the way a thermostat heats a room toward the temperature you set.

target = home pose + action

Predict

The brain gives the left knee a new target. When does the knee get there?

Check your prediction

A little later, and not quite exactly. Real motors are not instant: they lag behind their orders, their gears have a little slack, and they get weaker as the battery runs down and as they spin faster. The simulator models the lag and the weakening, and every main task has a twin that adds the gear slack, because at this size the motor is most of the difference between pretend and real. A brain trained on an instant, perfect motor would fall on the real one.

Try it for real

In Studio, write a motion by posing the robot and editing keyframes, and every angle is checked against the robot's joint travel. The preview interpolates your poses but does not run the motors' physics, which is what the bench is for.

For parents and teachers

The robot has 15 motors, and the fifteenth, which opens the beak, is one the walking brains never move.

The motor model in training is BAM, from Rhoban, which models a Dynamixel XL330's voltage, the back-EMF that weakens a spinning motor, and its friction. Book 3, lesson 6 goes inside it.

Something in this lesson unclear, or wrong? Tell us about it.

Where these numbers come from

  • The order: the README's joint layout puts joints 0 to 4 in the left leg (hip_yaw, hip_roll, hip_pitch, knee, ankle), 5 to 8 in the neck and head (neck_pitch, head_pitch, head_yaw, head_roll) and 9 to 13 in the right leg (microduck_rl README).
  • The target: "home pose + scale × action" (robotd design). The gear play in the backlash tasks is "±1° of gear play (2° total)", and "actuator fidelity is most of the sim2real gap" (README).
  • 15 motors: "a 50 Hz control loop driving fifteen servos" (microduck README).

Lesson 4 · The policy

A brain made of sums

The duck's brain is a policy: a function that turns 61 numbers into 14. Pollen's is a small neural network: 61 inputs, three layers of 512, 256 and 128 units, then 14 outputs.

Each unit multiplies its inputs by weights, adds them up with a bias, and bends the total with a simple curve. That is all a unit does.

Before the numbers go in, each is shifted and scaled by the average and spread it had in training, so a joint angle and a spin speed arrive on a similar scale. That rescaling is part of the brain, so it is built into the file the robot runs.

61 → 512 → 256 → 128 → 14

Predict

Training changes the brain. What exactly changes?

Check your prediction

The weights and biases. About 198,000 of them, set by training, are the difference between a duck that falls and one that walks, while the shape is chosen before training and never changes. Training turns the dials, and keeps the rescaling's averages up to date.

Try it for real

Open a policy in Studio's Behaviours: with every tool on, its detail shows the network's operations, layer widths, parameter count and tensor names. A file you bring yourself shows the same, or the exact reason it was refused.

For parents and teachers

The curve each unit uses is called ELU, and without a curve the three layers would collapse into one big multiplication, so the network could only draw straight lines.

The network actually outputs 14 averages: while practising, the duck tries numbers around them, by a spread it also learns for each motor, but on the robot the average is used.

Something in this lesson unclear, or wrong? Tell us about it.

Where these numbers come from

  • The shape: hidden_dims=(512, 256, 128), activation="elu", obs_normalization=True (the walking task's settings). The rescaling: "The exporter bakes the observation normalizer into the ONNX graph" (README).
  • About 198,000: 61 × 512 + 512, plus 512 × 256 + 256, plus 256 × 128 + 128, plus 128 × 14 + 14 is 197,774. One dot for 8 units is this picture's choice.

Lesson 5 · The reward

Points for what you want

Nobody writes the walk. They write the score, and reinforcement learning (RL) finds a walk that earns it.

The walking duck's score is a sum of terms, worked out every step. It earns points for matching the speed it was asked for, keeping its body upright, lifting its feet and pointing its head where asked. It loses points for jerky actions, sliding its feet, and its legs knocking into its body.

Get the score wrong and the duck finds the loophole, the way a student can learn the test instead of the subject.

"RL optimizes the letter of the reward."

Predict

The duck loses points for every tiny head wobble, and walking always makes its head wobble. What does training find?

Check your prediction

It stands still. When its trainers charged for every wobble of its heavy head, the best score came from not walking at all. The wobble could not be escaped while walking, so stopping was the cheapest way to avoid the charge. The fix charges only for the steady droop the duck can correct, so a good walk pays nothing for it.

Try it for real

Studio's Ball challenge reports the nine reward terms the kicking policies were trained on, so you can watch an edit move them. It ranks on whether the ball moved, not on the reward, because a leaderboard sorted on a reward "would reward a duck that stands beautifully still".

For parents and teachers

How much each term counts in the picture: speed 2.0, turning 2.0, upright 2.0, feet in the air 3.0, pose 1.0 and head 2.0 pay; jerky actions cost from 0.1 rising to 1.0 as training goes on, sliding feet 0.1, and legs knocking the body 1.0.

The head story is in the code: a stricter head term made the duck stop walking "by iter 300". The fix was "L1 on a 1 s EMA of the error", which charges the average droop rather than each wobble.

Where these numbers come from

  • How much each term counts, the curriculum from 0.1 to 1.0, the head story and "Standing still scored higher, so it stood still" are all in the walking task's settings.
  • The rule on the page is from Pollen's AGENTS.md. The nine terms and the still duck are in Studio's docs.

Lesson 6 · Training

Practice, at scale

Training runs 4,096 pretend ducks at once on a graphics card. Each duck takes 24 steps, then the brain learns from all of them together, and that is one iteration: 98,304 steps of experience.

A usable walk takes about 1 to 2 hours. Pollen's guide says simple tricks need about 1,000 iterations, while walking and getting up need 4,000 to 6,000, and a copy of the brain is saved every 250 iterations.

Trainers watch the score climb, and they watch the duck too, because the score can climb while the trick never happens.

4,096 ducks × 24 steps = 98,304 steps per iteration

Predict

One iteration is 24 steps for each duck. At 50 steps a second, how much of each duck's life is that?

Check your prediction

About half a second: 24 steps at 50 a second is 0.48 seconds of each duck's life, but short bursts from thousands of ducks give the brain a wide view of what it is doing.

Try it for real

With an NVIDIA graphics card and uv, you can train the walk:

git clone https://github.com/pollen-robotics/microduck_rl
cd microduck_rl
uv run train Mjlab-Velocity-Flat-MicroDuck --env.scene.num-envs 4096

No graphics card? Add --hf-jobs to run it on Hugging Face Jobs, billed to your Hugging Face account, and always do a smoke test first: --env.scene.num-envs 64 --agent.max_iterations 5.

For parents and teachers

The walking settings allow up to 50,000 iterations, a ceiling rather than a target: in September 2026, the walking brain Pollen had deployed was traced to a checkpoint at iteration 3,750.

Watching the duck is a rule in Pollen's guide: "Report what rollouts actually show", not "it works!".

Where these numbers come from

  • 4,096 ducks and 1 to 2 hours: "~1-2 h for a usable gait at 4096 envs" (README). 24 steps, saves every 250 and the 50,000 ceiling: num_steps_per_env=24, save_interval=250, max_iterations=50_000 (walking settings).
  • The budgets, "walk = 441tzs6d@3750", the climbing score and the smoke test: AGENTS.md. 98,304 is 4,096 times 24; 0.48 is 24 divided by 50.

Lesson 7 · The update

Small steps uphill

While it practises, the brain tries numbers around its average, so every action has a chance. After each round of practice it is nudged so that actions that went better than expected become more likely, and actions that went worse become less likely.

The method is PPO, proximal policy optimization, and proximal means near: it will not move the policy far in one go. Once a change would make an action more than 20% more or less likely than before, the update stops pushing it.

Past a 20% change in an action's likelihood, an update gains nothing more.

Predict

Why does PPO take small steps?

Check your prediction

Its practice came from the old brain. Change the brain too much and that practice no longer describes it, and learning falls apart. Small steps keep the new brain close enough to the old one that its practice still counts. Many small steps climb further than one wild leap.

Try it for real

In a training run's logs, watch the KL divergence, a measure of how far each update moved the policy: the walking task's learning rate adapts itself to keep it near its target.

For parents and teachers

Each batch of practice is reused 5 times, in 4 slices, before new practice is collected. A small bonus for staying unpredictable, called entropy, keeps the duck exploring instead of settling early.

"Better than expected" needs an expectation, so a second network, the critic, learns how much reward to expect from each moment. Book 3, lesson 2 opens it.

Something in this lesson unclear, or wrong? Tell us about it.

Where these numbers come from

  • 20%: clip_param=0.2. Reused 5 times in 4 slices: num_learning_epochs=5, num_mini_batches=4. The adaptive rate: schedule="adaptive", desired_kl=0.01. Entropy: entropy_coef=0.01 (walking settings).

Lesson 8 · Sim to real

Train on many worlds

A brain trained on one perfect world learns that world, flaws and all, so every pretend duck gets a slightly different one. Some differences are drawn once for each duck and some again each time it starts over, and the idea is called domain randomization.

The floor's grip ranges from 0.7 to 1.3 times normal. The main body's weight changes by 5% either way, and its balance point shifts by up to 15 mm. Every 3 to 6 seconds the duck is shoved, by up to 0.3 m/s. Its tilt sensor is mounted wrong by up to 6°, and each joint reading is off by a small, fixed amount.

A brain that walks in all of these treats the real robot as one more variation.

Train on a spread of worlds, so the real one falls inside it.

Predict

The real duck's tilt sensor sits 5° off, always the same way. Does the randomization take care of that?

Check your prediction

No: it has to be corrected on the robot. Each pretend duck's sensor is tipped a fixed amount in a random direction, so the brain learns to cope with an error of that size, not with one steady tilt that every duck shares. Pollen's training settings leave the real board's steady offset to be corrected on the robot, before the brain sees the numbers.

Try it for real

Studio's Evaluations walk a network from drop heights it was never tuned on and say exactly what varied between tries. That is the same idea from the other side: change the world, then measure.

For parents and teachers

Randomization is not free. Pollen once let the balance point shift by up to 30 mm, more than the 20 mm from ankle to heel, and walking got worse, so the cap is now 15 mm. Too wide a spread teaches a nervous walk.

The pushes used to reach 0.5 m/s, faster than the duck's top commanded walk of 0.4 m/s, which "trains a permanently nervous fall-recovery gait".

  • Every range, the 15 mm cap and its 30 mm history, the pushes and their history, and the 5° offset "corrected at the source in the runtime" are in the walking task's settings.
  • "IMU DR is zero-centered ... CANNOT compensate a systematic mounting bias" (AGENTS.md).

Lesson 9 · Deployment

From training to a robot

A trained brain leaves the simulator as a file, and every step from there is a check.

Export writes an ONNX file, a format many programs can run. Rehearse drives that file in a separate simulator on an ordinary computer, with the same motor model. Publish checks the file takes 61 numbers and gives 14, refuses one that outputs nonsense or a constant, and uploads it with a manifest that says how to run it.

On a robot, one command installs it and another runs it, and on a phone, Microduck Studio opens the same file and shows you what is inside.

train → export → rehearse → publish → install

Predict

Why must the file be exported with the project's own script, never converted by hand?

Check your prediction

The rescaling. Export writes the file with the input rescaling built in. Training's own playback applies it anyway, so the brain looks fine there. A file without it fails wherever it runs alone, on the robot or in the rehearsal script, because the brain sees numbers of the wrong size. That is why there is exactly one safe path.

Try it for real

uv run scripts/export.py Mjlab-Velocity-Flat-MicroDuck --wandb-run-path <...>
uv run scripts/infer_policy.py --walking output.onnx --new-cmd-obs
uv run publish --onnx output.onnx --repo <user>/microduck-<name> --kind perpetual --slot walk

Then on a robot, a new walk goes into the walk slot: sudo robotctl policy load walk <user>/microduck-<name>. In Studio, open the .onnx from Files or a direct link.

For parents and teachers

Repos are created private by default, and publishing will not overwrite an existing file without being told to. Studio checks a network's input and output widths and says exactly why it refuses one.

No robot yet? Pollen's microduck repository has a simulated duck, scripts/duck-sim, that runs the robot's real daemons against a body in MuJoCo.

Lesson 10 · Your project

Your first project: bounce a ball

A good first project changes as little as possible. Here is one to try: teach the duck to bounce a small ball off its beak.

Start from what exists. Pollen's kicking task already puts a 70 mm, 15 g ball in front of the duck, and the ground-pick task already bends it to touch the floor with its beak.

Change one thing at a time. The kicker cannot see the ball, but bouncing needs to know where it is, so the brain needs new inputs.

Then expect loopholes: Pollen's guide expects 2 to 5 rounds of fixing them.

Copy the closest task. Change one thing. Watch what it does.

Predict

You reward the duck for every step the ball is high above it. What might it learn?

Check your prediction

To hold it still. Balancing the ball on its head keeps it high every step, which pays more than any bounce. So reward each upward bounce off the beak, not the ball being high: pay for the bounce itself, the moment the beak sends the ball up.

Try it for real

On paper first: write your bounce reward in plain words, then predict its loophole, the laziest way a duck could score it.

If you code: clone microduck_rl, copy microduck_ball_kick_env_cfg.py to a new task, register it, and run the 5-iteration smoke test at 64 envs before anything long. Share what you find in an issue on this site's repository.

For parents and teachers

An honest limit: the kicking brain is never shown the ball, so "the operator aims the robot at the ball". A brain that needs the ball's position will run in simulation until the robot can see it, and a brain whose inputs are not the shared 61 cannot be swapped in by the robot's runtime as it is.

The kicker's critic does see the ball, during training only. Book 3, lesson 2 explains why that helps.
