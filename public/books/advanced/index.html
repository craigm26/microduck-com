Training Microducks

The reinforcement learning behind a Microduck's walk, with the settings in Pollen Robotics' training code, and the honest measuring that has to come after. Ten lessons, each with a figure to run.

Who it is for: makers, students and club leaders who can read a little algebra and want to train a policy, or judge one. Code helps but is not required. Book 2 has the same ideas without the formulas.

Lesson 1 · The problem

States, actions, rewards, return

Training a Microduck is set up as a Markov decision process. Markov means the next moment depends only on this one and the action. Strictly it is a partially observed one, since the policy sees an observation rather than the whole state. Every tick t the policy reads an observation ot of 61 numbers, chooses an action at of 14 joint targets, physics advances, and the environment pays a reward rt. The policies run at 50 Hz, so a tick is 20 ms. A tick and a step mean the same thing here: one turn of this loop.

The policy is trained to raise the expected discounted return: every reward still to come, each weighted less the further away it is. With the discount γ = 0.99, a reward one tick further away weighs 0.99 times as much.

Gt = rt + γrt+1 + γ2rt+2 + …, with a horizon of about 1/(1 − γ) ticks.

Predict

With γ = 0.99 at 50 Hz, roughly how far into the future does the policy look?

Check your prediction

About 100 ticks, 2 s. 1/(1 − γ) = 100 ticks is the policy's working horizon: about 2 s of the future. A reward 100 ticks away weighs 0.99100 ≈ 0.37 of one paid now. The horizon is soft: a reward 200 ticks away still counts 0.13, but the further off a payoff sits, the less it shapes the policy.

Try it for real

The discount sits in the walking task's runner settings, beside the other PPO numbers in this book:

gamma=0.99,
lam=0.95,
desired_kl=0.01,

Change it in the runner settings, train two full runs of microduck_rl, and compare the gaits. A smoke test only checks that a task builds and runs; it learns no gait. Every run, even a smoke test, needs a CUDA GPU or Hugging Face Jobs.

For teachers and mentors

Have the group compute 0.99k for k = 10, 50, 100 and 300 in a spreadsheet, then imagine a duck with γ = 0.9: its horizon is 10 ticks, 0.2 s. What could it never learn to set up?

The horizon is a property of the training objective, not of the network: the deployed policy just maps an observation to an action.

Where these numbers come from

  • γ = 0.99: "gamma=0.99" in the walking task's settings.
  • 50 Hz: policies "are trained here at 50 Hz" (microduck_rl README). 61 and 14: "obs[1,61] → actions[1,14]" (robotd design).
  • 20 ms, 0.37, 100 ticks and 2 s are arithmetic on those two numbers, as are 10 ticks and 0.2 s for γ = 0.9, and 0.13 at 200 ticks.

Lesson 2 · Credit

Value, advantage and GAE

PPO, the method in lesson 3, trains two networks. The actor is the policy. The critic learns V(s), the return the actor can expect from state s, so each action is judged against what was expected rather than against zero.

That judgment is the advantage. One step of it is the temporal-difference (TD) error δt. Generalized advantage estimation blends the errors that follow, each weighted by (γλ)k. Microduck uses λ = 0.95: a low λ leans on the critic's guesses, a high one on what really happened.

In the walking task only the critic sees the trunk's linear velocity, and every signal clean and on time; in the ball kick, only the critic sees the ball.

δt = rt + γV(st+1) − V(st), and Ât = Σk (γλ)k δt+k

Predict

The ball-kick critic sees the ball and the actor does not. What runs on the robot?

Check your prediction

Only the actor. The critic exists only in training, so it is allowed to cheat. The exported graph is the actor, 61 inputs to 14 outputs. The critic's privileged inputs never have to exist on the robot, which is exactly why it can be given them.

Try it for real

Find the privileged terms in the training code. The walking task gives one to the critic alone:

# Add base_lin_vel to critic only (privileged information)
cfg.observations["critic"].terms["base_lin_vel"] = ...

The ball kick's docstring explains its choice: the robot has no ball sensing, so the actor is ball-blind and the critic sees the ball.

For teachers and mentors

Give each pair a row of rewards and a guessed V. Compute δ by hand for a few steps, then Â with λ = 0 and with λ = 1. The first is one-step TD; the second is the sampled return minus V.

Asymmetric actor-critic is a general trick: train with what the simulator knows, deploy with what the robot senses.

Each advantage sums at most 24 sampled ticks, one rollout; the critic's estimate stands in for everything after. That is how the horizon of lesson 1 reaches past the end of a rollout.

Where these numbers come from

  • λ = 0.95: "lam=0.95" in the walking task's settings, which also say "Add base_lin_vel to critic only (privileged information)".
  • The ball: "The CRITIC does see ball pos/vel (asymmetric actor-critic)" (ball kick settings).
  • 61 and 14: robotd design. λ = 0 and 1 are the two ends of the range.

Lesson 3 · The update

PPO: better, but not too much

Each PPO iteration collects a batch with the current policy, then makes several passes over it. Microduck's runner takes 24 steps from each of 4,096 environments, 98,304 transitions, and makes 5 epochs over them in 4 minibatches.

Reusing data is risky: the policy could drift far from the one that collected it. PPO measures the drift per action with the ratio ρ = πnew(a|o) / πold(a|o), and its clipped objective stops paying once ρ leaves 1 ± ε in the helpful direction. Microduck uses ε = 0.2.

L = E[ min( ρÂ, clip(ρ, 1 − ε, 1 + ε) Â ) ], with ε = 0.2

Predict

An action had a positive advantage, and the update has already raised its ratio to 1.3. What does pushing it further earn?

Check your prediction

Nothing. For Â > 0 the objective is ρÂ only up to 1 + ε = 1.2, then flat, with zero gradient. For Â < 0 it flattens below 0.8.

Try it for real

On a run, plot the KL and the learning rate together: when the KL runs hot, the adaptive schedule backs the rate off. The settings, from the walking task's runner:

clip_param=0.2,
entropy_coef=0.01,
num_learning_epochs=5,
num_mini_batches=4,
learning_rate=1.0e-3,
schedule="adaptive",

Around the clip, the runner sets the rest. The learning rate starts at 0.001 and adapts: it is cut when the KL divergence runs well above its 0.01 target, and raised when it runs well below. The entropy bonus is 0.01. The policy is a Gaussian whose noise starts at a standard deviation of 1.0, and gradients are clipped at norm 1.0.

For teachers and mentors

Draw min(ρÂ, clip(ρ, 0.8, 1.2)Â) for Â = 1 and Â = −1 on squared paper. Ask which side of each curve goes flat, and why that is the safe side.

The entropy bonus is a reward for staying uncertain. Without it a policy can stop exploring before it has found a gait.

Where these numbers come from

  • ε, epochs, minibatches, learning rate, KL target, entropy, starting noise, gradient norm and 24 steps: the runner settings in the walking task.
  • 4,096 environments: "a usable gait at 4096 envs" (microduck_rl README).
  • 98,304 is 4,096 × 24; 20 is 5 × 4; 1.2 and 0.8 are 1 ± 0.2. The 1.3 in the question is an example.

Lesson 4 · The interface

61 numbers in, 14 out

Every Microduck policy has one shape, obs[1,61] → actions[1,14]. The robot's daemon builds one observation for all of them, in this order: gyro (3), projected gravity (3), joint positions (14), joint velocities (14), last action (14) and command (13). The shape never changes, so the runtime can hot-swap walk, stand and trick policies. In training, an action is a joint target: the home pose plus 1.0 × the output. The daemon's defaults differ: it scales walking actions by 0.9 and smooths the head and leg targets, so Pollen's two repositories do not agree on this yet.

Training copies the robot's flaws on purpose: joint velocity arrives 1 tick late, the IMU (the motion sensor) lags 0 to 1 tick, joint readings carry a fixed bias of up to ±0.015 rad, and the IMU a mounting tilt of up to 6°.

The same 61 inputs and 14 outputs, in the same order, for every policy.

Predict

Your new trick never uses the head command. What do you do with its 4 inputs?

Check your prediction

Keep them, with small sampled values. Deleting them breaks the contract, so unused command slots stay in. Pollen's notes say to sample small values in them from step 0, because a command input that is never non-zero keeps dead weights forever; the ball kick, for one, feeds plain zeros. Big random noise is not small, and no robot will ever send it.

Try it for real

In Microduck Studio, Probe this network lets you change one input of a policy and compare the actions; an input's z-score shows how far it sits from the training distribution.

On a computer, rehearse the runtime's swaps with Pollen's tool:

uv run scripts/infer_policy.py --walking walk.onnx \
  --standing stand.onnx --sitstand sitstand.onnx \
  --roulade roulade.onnx --new-cmd-obs

For teachers and mentors

Lay the 61 slots out on squared paper with their index ranges. The command is velocity 3, head 4 and body 6, at indices 48 to 60, as the daemon's own notes list it. Joint velocity is a tick old because the servo firmware computes it as a moving average.

The actor behind the plug is small: hidden layers of 512, 256 and 128 units, 197,774 weights and biases in all.

Where these numbers come from

  • The order, the sizes and indices 48 to 60: "[ gyro(3) | projected_gravity(3) | joint_pos(14) | joint_vel(14) | last_action(14) | command(13) ]" and "command = vel(3) + head(4) + body(6)" (robotd design).
  • Scale 1.0, the lags, the bias and the tilt: the walking task's settings. The daemon's 0.9: "Unset resolves per mode: 0.9 walking" (robotd.toml). Dead weights: AGENTS.md.
  • 197,774 is 61 × 512 + 512 + 512 × 256 + 256 + 256 × 128 + 128 + 128 × 14 + 14.

Lesson 5 · Rewards

The reward is the specification

RL optimizes the letter of the reward. Pollen's training notes keep hard-won rules:

  • Get the signs right. A cost that returns 0 or more takes a negative weight. A penalty that is already negative, returning 0 or less, takes a positive one. Mix them up and the policy is paid to misbehave.
  • No jackpots, and no pay for a bad state. Limit how often a "reach X" reward can pay. Never pay for just being in a bad state; pay the change in a potential instead, a score like cos(tilt). Rising pays, holding still pays zero.
  • Judge a term by its share of the total. The same weight counts a quarter as much when the task reward is 4 times bigger.

Pay progress, γΦ(st+1) − Φ(st), not presence.

Predict

A get-up task pays a point every tick the duck is low and trying. What does PPO find?

Check your prediction

Staying low: the cheapest qualifying state gets parked in and farmed.

Try it for real

Turn the sign rule into a test before training. microduck_rl's workflow asks for config tests that check "reward weights have the intended sign" and run on a CPU:

uv run --with pytest pytest tests/

Then, on every run, look at each Episode_Reward/<penalty> curve: all of them must stay at or below zero.

For teachers and mentors

Run a reward-hacking round: each group writes a reward for a trick in plain words, and another group finds the laziest way to score it. The training notes' own catches include "ballistic whip instead of a roll" and "head-tripod instead of standing".

Potential-based shaping pays γΦ(s′) − Φ(s). With γ in it, every path between two states earns the same discounted total, so the best behaviour stays the best (Ng, Harada and Russell, 1999). Pollen's upright reward pays the plain change, which is close when γ is near 1. One more rule from the same notes: add smoothness penalties only after the skill exists, or doing nothing wins.

Where these numbers come from

  • Every rule here, the zero check and the 4× example are from the reward design section of AGENTS.md.
  • The figure's totals are invented for the picture.

Lesson 6 · Hardware

The actuator is most of the gap

A Microduck is about 800 g of robot. Its policies drive 14 small Dynamixel XL330 servos, and a 15th opens the beak. Pollen's training repository says it plainly: at this scale, "actuator fidelity is most of the sim2real gap."

So instead of an ideal PD controller, a spring and damper toward the target that always gets the torque it asks for, training models each servo with BAM's M6 model: the voltage control law, back-EMF, and Coulomb, Stribeck and load-dependent friction. Back-EMF makes torque fall as the motor speeds up; friction makes a slow joint stick, then break away. Each environment draws its own battery voltage, sag, command delay and friction.

Backlash variants add ±1° of gear play, 2° in all, at every servo joint, read through the play as the real encoder on the output side reads it.

A servo is not an ideal PD controller: its torque falls with speed, and it sticks when slow.

Predict

The battery runs down. What happens to the line of torque the servo can give at each speed?

Check your prediction

The whole line drops. A lower battery voltage leaves less torque at every speed, so a tired battery makes a weaker servo. An ideal controller's flat line would not move. The actuator is most of the gap, so the simulator models the XL330 down to its voltage control law, and each environment draws its own battery voltage.

Try it for real

Rehearse one policy on a computer with and without the servo model. Pollen's rehearsal tool uses the BAM actuators by default; --no-bam falls back to plain PD actuators, and --vin pins the battery voltage:

uv run scripts/infer_policy.py --walking out.onnx --new-cmd-obs
uv run scripts/infer_policy.py --walking out.onnx --new-cmd-obs --no-bam

Keep --new-cmd-obs: without it the tool builds the older 51-number observation, and a 61-input policy is refused.

For teachers and mentors

Compare a spring with a heavy box. A spring pushes back the moment you move it, like an ideal controller. A box does not move at all until you push hard enough, then lurches: that is friction, and slow joints live in it.

BAM is Rhoban's open project, "better actuator models".

Where these numbers come from

  • About 800 g, the sim2real sentence, the BAM model, the randomized quantities and the backlash: the microduck_rl README.
  • 14 Dynamixel XL330 servos: AGENTS.md. Fifteen servos in all: robotd design. The 51-number observation: scripts/infer_policy.py.
  • The figure's curves are drawn to show their shapes only.

Lesson 7 · Robustness

Randomize, then measure

A simulator is wrong in ways nobody can list, so the walking task trains across many wrong worlds. Each duck keeps its own foot friction (×0.7 to 1.3) and trunk mass and inertia (±5%); each reset redraws the trunk's center of mass (up to ±15 mm) and joint friction (×0.9 to 1.1); and pushes of ±0.3 m/s come every 3 to 6 s.

Randomizing is a hypothesis; measuring is its own step. Studio tested cards meant to walk one square, played in rows (a row, a program of Duck Cards), with Pollen's v8 policy in duck-sim, under test ranges of its own fixed first: 95 to 98% of short rows landed within 10 cm and 15°, rows of 10 cards or more only 33 to 42%. duck-sim drives plain position servos, not lesson 6's servo model.

Randomize what you cannot know; measure against criteria fixed before looking.

Predict

Why do long rows miss so much more often than short ones?

Check your prediction

Drift. The duck's belief about where it is drifts with time and with distance walked, so a longer row ends further off. Studio split long rows into chapters, parts of up to 7 cards, with the duck re-seated between them on a nest, the square where the child sets it back down. That lifted long rows from 42% to 63%. A mat under each nest, printed for the duck's head camera to read as a compass, took them to 97.5% in simulation.

Try it for real

Read the evidence page from its criteria to its limits, then follow its reproduce section. Every raw row is published.

In the training code, most randomizations are ENABLE_* switches at the top of the task's settings, with their ranges beside them; the battery, sag and delay ranges live in microduck_constants.py.

For teachers and mentors

Before any test, have the group write down what "walks one square reliably" means, in numbers. Then compare with the evidence page's: 95% of rows within 10 cm, 95% within 15°, a fall bound of 1%, and time limits.

Note what is still open: the mat result is simulation only, and closed-loop cards stay off on real ducks. And note the randomizer rule: a draw that accumulated across resets once degraded every long training run for months.

Where these numbers come from

  • Friction, center of mass, mass, joint friction and pushes: the walking task's settings. The accumulating randomizer: AGENTS.md.
  • Every landing rate, the criteria, the 10 Hz rate and the chapters: the evidence page.

Lesson 8 · Evidence

Honest numbers

No falls in 100 rows (a row, a program of Duck Cards) does not mean a fall rate of zero. The Wilson 95% interval is a range that should hold the true rate 95 times in 100. For 0 falls in n rows it runs from 0 to z2/(n + z2), with z = 1.96. Bounding it at 1% takes 381 rows.

Rows are rarely independent, either. On the evidence page each condition's physics is drawn once per session of 12 rows, "so 400 rows are about 34 ducks", and intervals that count rows come out too narrow; later candidates were judged by a permutation test over sessions.

And decide what counts first: those criteria were written down on 2026-10-15, "before any result was in, and not changed since."

For 0 events in n trials, the Wilson 95% upper bound is z2/(n + z2), with z = 1.96.

Predict

No falls in 100 rows. What is the upper end of the Wilson 95% interval on the fall rate?

Check your prediction

About 3.7%: 1.962 / (100 + 1.962) = 0.037, so the interval runs up to 3.7% at n = 100. The rule of three, 3/n, a one-sided bound, says 3%.

Try it for real

The bound for zero events is one line of Python:

z = 1.96
for n in (100, 381, 1000):
    print(n, z * z / (n + z * z))

Then read how the evidence page compares candidates session by session rather than row by row.

For teachers and mentors

Before an experiment, have the group write the criteria and the analysis on paper, seal it, and open it afterward. Pre-registration is that envelope.

The rule of three is a quick check for zero events; Wilson is what the evidence page uses.

Where these numbers come from

  • "the upper end of the Wilson 95% interval on the fall rate is at most 1% (with zero falls, that takes 381 rows)", the sessions of 12, the 400 rows and 34 ducks, the permutation test and the date: the evidence page.
  • 3.7%, 0.037, 3% and the 10% for 34 ducks are arithmetic; 1.96 is the normal quantile a two-sided 95% interval uses; 1000 in the snippet is an example.

Lesson 9 · People

Learning from people

Some of what makes a duck good company will not fit in a formula, so people supply it.

Episodes. Kith, a web game by this site's author, records an agent's own view five rows a second: what it sensed, what it did next, and its reward under a published schedule. It can release the walks people give as an open LeRobot dataset; so far its release holds only scripted walks.

Picks. Studio's Compare plays two runs side by side and you pick the better. The docs call it "the human-feedback half of RLHF", reinforcement learning from human feedback. Picks can train a scorer learned from picks, which can then shape a policy's reward.

Poses. Mimic puts the duck in the pose of whoever stands in front of the camera.

P(A picked over B) = σ(SA − SB), where S is a run's score and σ is the logistic curve, which turns a gap in score into a chance.

Predict

What do your picks in Compare train on your phone?

Check your prediction

A taste that re-weights Pollen's reward. With enough picks, Studio's Train from your picks fits your taste on the phone: a simple scorer learned from picks. It then scales Pollen's reward terms up or down, within bounds, for a training run off the phone, so it re-weights the reward Pollen wrote rather than replacing it. Settings decides whether the picks may be shared.

Try it for real

Walk with a duck in Kith; its published rewards name the skill each one is meant for, and several say no policy exists yet. Then judge two moves in Studio's Compare, and make one with Mimic.

For teachers and mentors

Run a pick tournament: two moves on a screen, a show of hands, a tally. Then ask what the tally would teach a scorer, and what it would miss.

A scorer learned from picks inherits the pickers' blind spots. A policy trained against it can exploit them, exactly like a hand-written reward.

Where these numbers come from

  • "a table of your own view at five rows a second" and the open LeRobot dataset: Kith's notes for agents. The scripted first release: Kith's dataset notes.
  • Compare, picks and Settings: Studio's docs. Mimic: Duck Cards.

Lesson 10 · Shipping

Ship it, and say what it does

Every gate is cheap next to a wasted run or a fallen robot:

  1. Smoke test: 64 environments, 5 iterations; it "catches ~95% of config errors for cents".
  2. Config tests on a CPU: indices, signs, gates.
  3. Train and read the run: penalties ≤ 0, the task term growing.
  4. Export with scripts/export.py, normalizer baked into the ONNX.
  5. Rehearse in scripts/infer_policy.py, swaps and all.
  6. Publish: checked, then uploaded, private until you say so.
  7. Measure where runs can be reset and scored; share the EvalLog, one file that keeps the results beside the whole specification.

Rollouts are the policy's real runs: "Report what rollouts actually show." Whoever asked decides when it is good enough.

Predict

Publishing refuses your policy. What could the reason be?

Check your prediction

NaN outputs. Publishing checks the shape, [1,61] → [1,14], and refuses a file with NaN or constant output. Whether the policy is good is a measurement, an EvalLog, not a publishing check.

Try it for real

The whole path, with Pollen's tools:

uv run train <TASK_ID> --env.scene.num-envs 64 --agent.max_iterations 5
uv run --with pytest pytest tests/
uv run train <TASK_ID> --env.scene.num-envs 4096
uv run scripts/export.py <TASK_ID> --wandb-run-path <...>
uv run scripts/infer_policy.py --walking output.onnx --new-cmd-obs
uv run publish --onnx output.onnx --repo <user>/microduck-<name> \
  --kind perpetual --slot walk --dry-run

That publishes a walk. A trick publishes with --kind episodic --duration-s 4.0 instead. With a duck, sudo robotctl policy load walk puts a gait in the walk slot and sudo robotctl policy add installs a trick. Without one, Studio's evaluations score a network on a bench and write an EvalLog.

For teachers and mentors

Put the gates on the wall as a checklist, and make "what did the rollout actually show?" the first question at every review, before anyone says "it works".

An EvalLog carries its whole specification beside its results, so a number can be traced to the run that made it.

Where these numbers come from

  • 64 environments, 5 iterations and ~95%: "A 5-iteration smoke test at 64 envs catches ~95% of config errors for cents." "Report what rollouts actually show" and the smoke-test command are from the same AGENTS.md.
  • [1,61] → [1,14], NaNs, constant outputs, private repositories, the commands and their numbers: the microduck_rl README.
  • EvalLog: Studio's docs.
