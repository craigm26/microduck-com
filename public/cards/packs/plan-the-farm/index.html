How it is scored.

Fewest cards wins. Count every card between Start and End: Repeat and End repeat, and every card under another. A Your card is one card. This is where a loop pays off.

Par is the shortest row the pack's author found. If your class finds a shorter one, the par was wrong, not the class.

A row solves a challenge when the duck ends on the goal, has walked through every tunnel the long way, and has not bumped into a wall or the edge of the board. When a challenge names a card to finish with, that card is last. Every Repeat needs its End repeat.

The board.

Every challenge card below draws its own board. Here is how big a square is.

The board rule

One Walk card moves the duck one square. One Turn card is a quarter turn, where it stands. That is the rule on paper and in Studio, and every par is counted by it.

A real duck

Walk and Turn cards on a duck are timed for now: two seconds each, and the duck does not land on board squares. Measured cards are being tested. So for now the board is played by people and in Studio, and a duck beside it is a trial.

Measured cards

Measured cards walk until the duck's own odometry says one 30 cm square and turn until it says a quarter turn. They are being validated, in Pollen's simulator and then on testers' ducks, before they are switched on.

Squares for people

Tape the board on the floor in 30 cm squares, so a student playing the duck can stand in one. Or draw it on squared paper and walk a finger.

Lessons.

Each one fits a class period with paper only.

Your cards

Name a routine

Walk the row, Row end right and Row end left are Your cards: printed cards saved under one name. Write out every card The whole field stands for, step by step, and count them. Then count the cards you laid.

Ask: when is a run of cards worth a name of its own?

Specifications

Write the spec first

Before laying cards, write in plain sentences what the route must do: where it starts and faces, where it ends, which gates it passes, what it must never bump, and the card it finishes with. A spec says what, not how. Trade specs with another group, and each writes a row for the other's spec.

Ask: did a row meet your spec and still surprise you? What did the spec leave out?

Limits

Forty steps

A duck runs at most 40 steps in one row. A full six by six field, row by row, takes 45 steps, so The whole field is five rows, which take 37. Plan the sixth row as a second run: mark a nest square where the first run ends, and a person sets the duck there, facing the right way, before the second run.

Ask: where would you split a field into runs, and why there?

Authoring

Start a level

Each group draws a six by six field on squared paper: a start and facing, a goal, walls, one or two gates, and a finish card if you like. Solve it with plain cards only, because a class pack Studio writes carries no Your cards. Your card count is your par. Trade levels and try to beat each other's par.

Ask: if another group beat your par, what did they see that you did not?

Robots and people

Real robots, real specs

Real field robots and milking robots follow plans that people write and check. A milking robot stays in one place and the cows walk to it. The duck only plays a game about these robots; it does no farm work. Write three more lines a real farm robot's spec would need.

Ask: what should a real farm robot do when a person or an animal is in its way?
