How it is scored.

Fewest cards wins. Count every card between Start and End: Repeat and End repeat, and every card under another. A Your card is one card. This is where a loop pays off.

Par is the shortest row the pack's author found. If your class finds a shorter one, the par was wrong, not the class.

A row solves a challenge when the duck ends on the goal, has walked through every tunnel the long way, and has not bumped into a wall or the edge of the board. When a challenge names a card to finish with, that card is last. Every Repeat needs its End repeat.

The board.

Every challenge card below draws its own board. Here is how big a square is.

The board rule

One Walk card moves the duck one square. One Turn card is a quarter turn, where it stands. That is the rule on paper and in Studio, and every par is counted by it.

A real duck

Walk and Turn cards on a duck are timed for now: two seconds each, and the duck does not land on board squares. Measured cards are being tested. So for now the board is played by people and in Studio, and a duck beside it is a trial.

Measured cards

Measured cards walk until the duck's own odometry says one 30 cm square and turn until it says a quarter turn. They are being validated, in Pollen's simulator and then on testers' ducks, before they are switched on.

Squares for people

Tape the board on the floor in 30 cm squares, so a student playing the duck can stand in one. Or draw it on squared paper and walk a finger.

Lessons.

Each one fits a class period with paper only.

Loops

Say it shorter

Solve Peck at the row end with plain cards, then again with Repeat and End repeat, writing the number on Repeat in pencil or on a sticky note. The score is cards: count every card between Start and End, Repeat and End repeat included. Print 2 Repeat and 2 End repeat cards for each group.

Ask: how many cards did Repeat save, and how many steps does the duck still take?

Setting up

A field on a table

Six by four squares of 30 cm is 1.8 by 1.2 m, too big for most classroom floors. Draw the field on a sheet of tabletop paper with small squares, and walk a finger or a toy along each row first. Save the big taped field for a run with the duck.

Ask: what changes when the squares are smaller, and what stays the same?

Debugging

Bump!

Hand out the buggy row for Bump at the row end. Groups act it out card by card and stop on the card that bumps. Find the one wrong card and fix the row with a single swap. Then do the same for Check the crops.

Ask: was the wrong card the one that bumped, or a card before it?

Patterns

Row by row

Trace Turn at the row end with a finger and say each move aloud. Circle the two long walks. Each one is a Repeat. The turn at the row end happens once, so it stays as plain cards between them.

Ask: which part of the path happens again, and which part happens only once?

Robots and people

What would a person check?

In Check the crops the duck pecks at the end, but the peck is only pretend: nothing on the duck can look at a plant. Real farmers walk their rows to look. Talk about what a person might look for, like holes in leaves, dry soil or a broken stem, and what a person does when something is wrong.

Ask: if a plant looked wrong, who would you tell, and what would you write down?
