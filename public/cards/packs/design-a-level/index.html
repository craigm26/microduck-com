How it is scored.

Fewest cards wins. Count every card between Start and End: Repeat and End repeat, and every card under another. A Your card is one card. This is where a loop pays off.

Par is the shortest row the pack's author found. If your class finds a shorter one, the par was wrong, not the class.

A row solves a challenge when the duck ends on the goal, has walked through every tunnel the long way, and has not bumped into a wall or the edge of the board. When a challenge names a card to finish with, that card is last. Every Repeat needs its End repeat.

The board.

Every challenge card below draws its own board. Here is how big a square is.

The board rule

One Walk card moves the duck one square. One Turn card is a quarter turn, where it stands. That is the rule on paper and in Studio, and every par is counted by it.

A real duck

Walk and Turn cards on a duck are timed for now: two seconds each, and the duck does not land on board squares. Measured cards are being tested. So for now the board is played by people and in Studio, and a duck beside it is a trial.

Measured cards

Measured cards walk until the duck's own odometry says one 30 cm square and turn until it says a quarter turn. They are being validated, in Pollen's simulator and then on testers' ducks, before they are switched on.

Squares for people

Tape the board on the floor in 30 cm squares, so a student playing the duck can stand in one. Or draw it on squared paper and walk a finger.

Lessons.

Each one fits a class period with paper only.

Your cards

Name a routine

Study Stairs to the nest. Stair step is a Your card: four printed cards saved under one number. Write out every card that Repeat 4, Stair step, End repeat stands for, and count how many cards it saves.

Ask: what makes a run of cards worth saving as a Your card?

Authoring

Write a level for another group

Each group draws a six by six board on squared paper: a start square and facing, a goal, up to six walls, one or two gates, and a finish card if you like. Write a row that solves it and count its cards. That count is your par. Trade boards with another group and play theirs.

Ask: what did the other group find hard about your level that you did not expect?

Checking par

Prove your par

Kick from the island was first written with par 9; another group solved it with 4 by letting the stairs keep going. Before trading a level, try hard to beat your own par. If someone else beats it, the par was wrong, not the players, so change the number.

Ask: how could you be sure no shorter row exists?

Search

Steps par by hand

A steps par can be found for certain: from the start, mark every square the duck can reach in one step, then two, then three, like ripples on a pond, until the goal is marked. Do it for one level on paper, then compare with the par the pack's checker computed.

Ask: why is a steps par easier to be sure of than a cards par?

Events

When it falls

Add a When it falls row to your solution: what the duck does once it is back on its feet. The board does not play it, but a duck running your row would. Test it by having the student playing the duck sit down when you say fall.

Ask: should the duck carry on after a fall, or go back to the start?
